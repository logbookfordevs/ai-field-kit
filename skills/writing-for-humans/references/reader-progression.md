# Reader Progression

Design the conditions for a reader to move from a plausible starting state to a useful outcome. The reader's state is a hypothesis to test, not an internal sequence the writer controls. “Reader journey” is useful editorial language for this practice, not a claim that one canonical framework governs reading.

Choose a progression from the reader's job. The shapes below synthesize reader-centered writing, rhetorical organization, discourse comprehension, document design, conceptual change, information foraging, and narrative research into working guidance. They are not required headings or universal psychological stages.

## Passage Map

Use this map at section scale when the path is not already obvious:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Entry | What must the reader know, believe, notice, or be able to do here? |
| Active question | What live question or task makes this passage relevant? |
| Work | Does it orient, explain, evidence, exemplify, compare, refute, concede, or enable? |
| Exit | What can the reader understand or do now that they could not before? |
| Blockage | Which missing inference, ambiguity, objection, or prerequisite could break the passage? |
| Check | What response or behavior would show that the exit is supported? |

Keep the map as working material unless the user asks to see it. Curiosity is motivation; tension may be emotion or structure; discovery is an experience; understanding and action are outcomes. They need not occur in a fixed order.

## Find or Do

Use for documentation, reference, service content, troubleshooting, and procedures.

```text
orientation -> answer or prerequisite -> common path -> decision branches -> recovery -> deeper reference
```

- Put the answer or first useful action where the reader can find it quickly.
- Give headings and links strong information scent: say what is behind them.
- Keep prerequisites beside the step they gate.
- Place failure recovery where failure can occur.
- Let the reader leave once their task is complete; reading time is not the goal.

Check by asking the reader to find and use the answer, not to recall the page's wording.

## Build a Mental Model

Use for technical explanations, tutorials, and unfamiliar systems.

```text
familiar frame -> missing distinction -> mechanism -> worked example -> boundary or counterexample -> transfer
```

- Introduce prerequisite concepts before relying on them.
- Connect each example to the mechanism it demonstrates.
- Use a boundary or counterexample to prevent an overbroad rule.
- End with a new case when transfer matters.

Check whether the reader can explain the mechanism and use it beyond the original example. Recognition and paraphrase alone are weaker evidence.

## Revise a Misconception

Use when the starting conception is specific and verifiably inaccurate.

```text
state the existing conception fairly -> expose its explanatory failure -> refute it explicitly -> supply a plausible replacement -> discriminate or apply
```

- Make the replacement intelligible rather than merely declaring the earlier view wrong.
- Show why the new account explains the evidence better.
- Give the reader a case that distinguishes the old and new models.
- Use an argument pattern when the disagreement concerns values, goals, or trade-offs. A different preference is not a misconception.

Conceptual change is not reliably linear. A reader may understand the replacement without accepting it, retain both models, or revise only part of the original view.

## Evaluate or Decide

Use for proposals, recommendations, decision documents, and opinionated essays.

```text
decision and stakes -> criteria -> evidence -> alternatives and trade-offs -> conditional judgment -> consequence or next action
```

- State the decision or live question before making the reader excavate its relevance.
- Separate evidence from the criteria used to judge it.
- Present the strongest material alternative and the conditions under which it wins.
- Make the conclusion proportional to the evidence.
- Let a reader reconstruct the reasoning while still disagreeing with the judgment.

Check whether the reader can connect evidence to the conclusion, name a limitation, and apply the criteria to another case.

## Follow a Case or Narrative

Use when events, choices, or consequences are themselves part of the explanation.

```text
context -> consequential tension -> evidence or attempt -> decision or discovery -> consequence -> useful agency
```

- Open with a promise the material will actually pay off.
- Preserve causal links between events rather than arranging facts for drama alone.
- Distinguish what one case illustrates from what the evidence establishes generally.
- Return agency at the end: an action, judgment, question, or durable lesson.

Narrative transportation can increase absorption without ensuring comprehension or factual accuracy. Reference material, urgent instructions, and review documents often need direct access more than suspense.

## Attention Without Friction

- **Curiosity:** Reveal a meaningful gap after the reader can recognize why closing it matters. A title that only withholds the subject creates fog.
- **Tension:** Use a contradiction, trade-off, failure, or changed condition already present in the material. Manufactured obstacles spend trust.
- **Surprise:** Let it revise significance or expose a hidden assumption. Decorative reversal makes the path harder to model.
- **Concrete detail:** Keep details that clarify mechanism, consequence, character, or stakes. Interesting but irrelevant detail competes with the intended model.
- **Pacing:** Vary density around difficulty. Put orientation before a demanding passage and relief after it; do not dilute a simple answer to simulate momentum.
- **Scanning:** Treat headings, openings, summaries, and links as promises. A reader who finds what they need and leaves has not failed the journey.

## Inspection and Validation

Use the strongest evidence available without overstating what it proves:

- **Text inspection:** determine whether the draft visibly provides the orientation, evidence, connections, examples, and recovery paths its intended exit requires.
- **Fresh-reader proxy:** ask an uninformed reader-agent to attempt the relevant check from the draft alone. This can reveal friction and missing support, but it does not establish how humans will respond.
- **Human validation:** observe the intended reader using, explaining, judging, recalling, or responding to the piece in a realistic situation.

Match the check to the promised exit:

| Intended exit | Inspection or fresh-reader proxy | Human validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Find | Locate the answer from a realistic entry point. | The intended reader finds and uses it. |
| Explain | Restate the mechanism and connect its parts. | The intended reader explains it accurately. |
| Distinguish | Classify a new case and reject the nearest alternative. | The intended reader distinguishes cases in context. |
| Evaluate | Reconstruct the evidence-to-judgment path and name a limitation. | The intended reader judges a realistic case with the criteria. |
| Apply | Attempt a new case without copying the worked example. | The intended reader solves the relevant new case. |
| Do | Follow the task, including likely failure and recovery. | The intended reader completes the real task. |
| Feel | Identify the intended movement and its textual cause. | The intended reader reports or displays the relevant response. |
| Remember | Identify the central model or image meant to survive. | The intended reader recalls it after the interval that matters. |

A heading skim tests navigation, not comprehension. Satisfaction tests feeling, not correctness. Agreement tests persuasion, not whether the argument was understood. Use several checks only when the piece promises several outcomes, and never describe inspection or a proxy read as human validation.
