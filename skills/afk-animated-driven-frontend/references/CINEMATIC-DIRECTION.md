# Cinematic Direction

Use film language to make interaction, framing, pacing, sound, and technical execution carry one idea. The vocabulary is a directing instrument, not decoration: every shot choice must change what the audience notices, feels, understands, or can do.

## Translate the medium

The browser is not a passive movie screen, so translate rather than imitate:

| Film language | Frontend equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| Frame | Viewport and active composition |
| Set | Page, scene, or rendered world |
| Subject or character | Product, user action, object, copy, or focal UI |
| Camera | Viewport, crop, scale, depth, focus, and spatial point of view |
| Edit | State transition, cut, scroll boundary, route change, or interaction consequence |
| Performance | User input and the interface's response |
| Coverage | Responsive, input, reduced-motion, and fidelity variants |
| Dailies | Rendered recordings or live cuts reviewed before expansion |
| Final cut | The user-approved experience, not merely the latest build |

Scrolling may drive a shot, move the camera, reveal an edit, or simply navigate. Decide which job it owns. A continuous page is not automatically a sequence shot, and parallax is not automatically cinematography.

## Charge the creative battery

Capture these inputs in the director's notebook:

- **Premise** — the experience in one sentence.
- **Audience-facing theme** — what makes the visitor care enough to enter.
- **Product truth** — the idea, value, or action the experience must ultimately prove.
- **Conflict and change** — what is unresolved at entry and meaningfully different after the journey.
- **Primary feeling** — the emotional register the cut should sustain or transform.
- **Reference frames** — films, animation, title sequences, games, sites, materials, sounds, or real places carrying relevant qualities.
- **Anti-references** — attractive directions that would betray this idea.
- **Non-negotiables** — content, interaction, brand, accessibility, or performance constraints that survive every treatment.

The agent uses these inputs to propose and execute; it does not average them into generic spectacle. References supply qualities and techniques, not surfaces to copy.

## Choose the preproduction order

Use **script first** when the message, argument, dialogue, or product explanation must govern the visuals. Let the shot list reinforce what the script needs the audience to know and feel.

Use **shot list or storyboard first** when visual discovery, spatial choreography, or an iconic transformation carries the idea. Let the script become narration, captions, or silence around the images.

Use both iteratively when neither can be judged alone. The required artifacts are the smallest set that lets the co-directors see the same intended film before expensive production begins:

- **Treatment** — tone, premise, audience journey, story spine, cinematographic thesis.
- **Script** — narration, interface copy, dialogue, beats, and deliberate silence.
- **Shot list** — framing, perspective, movement, subject action, transition, and dramatic job.
- **Storyboard or animatic** — visual sequence and approximate edit rhythm.
- **Technical scout** — driver, inputs, device floor, assets, warm path, fidelity, and signature risks.

## Direct with shots

Choose shot size by meaning:

| Shot | Frontend job |
| --- | --- |
| Establishing / extreme wide | Establish the world, system, route, or relationship before asking for detail. |
| Wide / long | Show the subject acting inside meaningful context. |
| Medium | Balance a product action with enough environment to understand cause and effect. |
| Close-up | Concentrate attention on one reaction, feature, decision, or emotionally important change. |
| Extreme close-up / detail | Make a tiny interaction, texture, cursor, mark, or state transition carry the beat. |
| POV / over-the-shoulder | Put the visitor inside the task or beside an acting user or agent. |
| Sequence shot | Preserve continuity through several beats without a perceptual cut when that continuity has meaning. |

Choose movement by dramatic job:

- **Static** lets action inside the frame speak and can create calm, tension, or confidence.
- **Pan or tilt** reveals a relationship or introduces information along an axis.
- **Tracking** follows a subject and makes its journey legible.
- **Dolly in or focus pull** intensifies attention; dolly out restores context or reveals consequence.
- **Arc or orbit** changes the audience's relationship to a subject while preserving continuity.
- **Cut, match cut, dissolve, or wipe** changes time, place, state, or meaning; the transition earns its form from that change.

A camera or viewport move must reveal, relate, intensify, reframe, transform, or hand off agency. Prefer a cut or static frame when movement has no dramatic job. Responsive design may need different coverage rather than a cramped crop of the desktop shot.

## Compose and light the frame

For every important shot, direct:

- **Subject** — the one element that owns attention.
- **Environment** — enough context to establish meaning without competing with the subject.
- **Depth and focus** — foreground, middle ground, background, occlusion, blur, scale, contrast, and focus order.
- **Lighting** — direction, intensity, and softness or hardness; use separation and contrast to clarify the subject.
- **Color** — a dominant field, supporting range, and restrained accent when appropriate. Correct tonal balance and legibility before grading for emotion.
- **Typography and graphic elements** — cast as performers in the frame, not labels pasted over the film.
- **Negative space** — room for anticipation, isolation, scale, or the next movement.

Color grading unifies an approved cut; it cannot rescue weak blocking, unclear hierarchy, or an unapproved visual world.

## Edit for attention and change

Direct rhythm through contrast:

- anticipation before reveal;
- hold before commitment;
- impact at the visible causal event;
- release after intensity;
- quiet frames around dense frames;
- wide context before meaningful detail;
- visual substitution when an image can carry what copy would over-explain.

The user controls part of the performance. Define how the edit behaves when they pause, reverse, scrub quickly, skip, resize, or return. Preserve a stable state at every interruption boundary.

## Direct sound and touch

Sound and haptics are production channels, not automatic polish. Design them beside the shot they serve:

- **Impact** marks a cut, collision, commit, reveal, or transformation.
- **Riser** builds toward a nearby climax.
- **Tonal one-shot** foreshadows or marks reflection without demanding immediate resolution.
- **Ambience** establishes place and continuity.
- **Silence** creates contrast, rest, or focus.
- **Haptic pulse** confirms contact or commitment when the platform permits it.

Anchor time-sensitive cues to the rendered event the audience perceives. Sound begins from intentional user action, remains mutable, and never carries required meaning alone. Haptics are brief and supplemental, never continuous ambience. When the user or organization maintains curated tool or asset libraries, consult them before inventing a new pipeline; every selected tool still passes the stack, licensing, performance, and accessibility gates.

## Run screenings, not reveals

Dailies should expose work early enough to change it. For each screening, provide:

1. the live cut or motion recording;
2. the approved treatment or shot it attempts;
3. the target device, input, motion preference, and fidelity tier;
4. the variants or compromises being compared;
5. the technical findings that constrain the direction;
6. one clear co-directing decision or request for notes.

The agent brings an opinion and explains it. The user may select, combine, reject, or redirect. Record the result before production continues.

## Cinematic acceptance

A sequence is ready for final-cut consideration when:

- the audience can state the premise and next action;
- the conflict-to-change relationship survives without explanatory commentary;
- framing, movement, edit, grade, sound, and touch have named dramatic jobs;
- the product remains useful before, during, and after the directed sequence;
- reverse, pause, skip, responsive, reduced-motion, muted, and low-fidelity coverage preserve meaning;
- rendered evidence supports the feel, while traces support the engineering;
- the user has explicitly approved the cut.
